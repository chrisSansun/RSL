Al-Anon and Family Support Groups - A Beginner's Guide

Al-Anon exists for one reason: addiction does not only happen to the person using. The family is affected just as profoundly, and often with far fewer resources. Here is what Al-Anon is, what it is not, and what to expect if you decide to try it.

Key takeaways

  • Al-Anon was founded in 1951 by Lois Wilson to support families affected by addiction, and operates in more than 130 countries with free membership and peer-led meetings.
  • Al-Anon differs from AA because it focuses on the family member's relationship with the addicted person and themselves, addressing concepts like detachment, enabling, and self-care specific to the family experience.
  • The 12-step framework adapted for families addresses powerlessness over the loved one's addiction and helps members develop acceptance and personal recovery regardless of the addicted person's actions.
  • Al-Anon meetings provide a confidential, peer-support format where members share experiences without advice-giving, offering powerful normalizing effects through recognition of shared struggles.
  • Research shows Al-Anon improves family members' psychological wellbeing, reduces their sense of responsibility for the addiction, and increases self-care behaviours according to a 2019 study in the Journal of Substance Abuse Treatment.

What Al-Anon is

Al-Anon Family Groups is a mutual support organisation for people whose lives have been affected by someone else's drinking or drug use. It was founded in 1951 by Lois Wilson - wife of Bill Wilson, co-founder of Alcoholics Anonymous - in recognition of the fact that families and friends of people with addiction were suffering in their own right and needed their own support.

Today Al-Anon has groups in more than 130 countries. Meetings take place in community centres, churches, online, and in hospitals. Membership is free. There are no professional facilitators - meetings are run by members, for members. The only requirement for attendance is that you have been affected by someone else's drinking or drug use.

Al-Anon is grounded in the 12-step tradition that underpins Alcoholics Anonymous, but it is a completely separate organisation with its own programme, literature, and structure. The person with the addiction does not attend Al-Anon. It exists specifically and exclusively for the people around them.

How it differs from AA

The confusion between AA and Al-Anon is common and worth clearing up directly. Alcoholics Anonymous is for people with alcohol use disorder. Al-Anon is for their families and friends. They share the 12-step framework and the meeting format, but the content is entirely different - because the experience of addiction is entirely different from the family side.

In AA, the work is about the member's relationship with alcohol. In Al-Anon, the work is about the member's relationship with the person who drinks - and with themselves. The concepts that Al-Anon focuses on - detachment, enabling, control, self-care, acceptance - are specific to the family experience of addiction and cannot be addressed within an AA framework.

The 12 steps for families

Al-Anon uses the same 12 steps as AA, adapted for the family experience. The steps address powerlessness over the loved one's addiction (Step 1), coming to accept that the family member cannot control or cure the addiction, making amends for one's own behaviour in the relationship, and developing a spiritual or personal framework for ongoing recovery.

The 12-step framework is not universally embraced - some members find the spiritual dimension off-putting, and Al-Anon accommodates a range of personal beliefs. The steps are a suggested programme, not a requirement. Members can attend meetings and benefit from the community without working through the steps formally.

What happens at a meeting

Al-Anon meetings follow a consistent format. A chairperson opens the meeting with readings from Al-Anon literature. Members share in turn - briefly, without cross-talk or advice-giving. What is heard in the meeting stays in the meeting. There is no pressure to speak; newcomers can simply listen. The meeting closes with a reading and, usually, a moment of connection among members.

The first meeting can be uncomfortable. The topic is personal, the room may contain strangers, and the format is unfamiliar. Most people who persist find that the discomfort of the first meeting gives way, over subsequent attendance, to something that feels more like recognition than exposure. Hearing other people describe experiences that match one's own - and describe them without shame - is a powerful normalising experience.

Online meetings

Al-Anon's online provision expanded significantly during the Covid pandemic and has remained robust. Video meetings are available in most languages and across most time zones. For people who cannot easily attend in-person meetings - due to geography, care responsibilities, or privacy concerns - online meetings offer the same programme content and peer connection as in-person attendance. The Al-Anon website provides a meeting finder.

Alateen - for younger family members

Alateen is the Al-Anon programme for teenagers aged 12 to 17 who have been affected by a family member's addiction. Meetings are facilitated by trained Al-Anon members and provide a peer community for young people whose experience of growing up in an addicted family is otherwise very difficult to talk about. Alateen meetings are available in many areas and online.

What Al-Anon is not

Understanding what Al-Anon does not do is as important as understanding what it does. Al-Anon is not therapy - it does not provide professional clinical support, and it should not be treated as a substitute for it. For family members dealing with significant trauma, depression, or anxiety, professional therapeutic support is appropriate alongside Al-Anon rather than instead of it.

Al-Anon will not fix the family member with the addiction. This is a point the programme makes explicitly: the only person any Al-Anon member can change is themselves. Al-Anon is not an intervention strategy, and attendance at meetings will not make the person with the addiction stop drinking or using. What it does is help the family member detach with love - to stop being consumed by the management of someone else's addiction, and to begin attending to their own life.

Does it work? The evidence base

Al-Anon has a more limited evidence base than some clinical interventions - partly because the peer-support format is difficult to study with the same rigour as a structured clinical trial. The studies that exist are generally positive. A substantial body of research shows that family involvement in recovery support - whether through Al-Anon or other mechanisms - improves outcomes both for the family member and, indirectly, for the person with the addiction.

More specifically, Al-Anon has been shown to improve family members' psychological wellbeing, reduce their sense of responsibility for the addiction, and increase their own self-care behaviours. A 2019 study published in the Journal of Substance Abuse Treatment found significant improvements in mental health and family functioning among Al-Anon participants. The evidence does not suggest it works for everyone - no single approach does - but it is well-supported as a low-barrier, freely available resource.

Getting started

To find a meeting, visit the Al-Anon Family Groups website and use the meeting finder. Meetings require no registration, no payment, and no commitment to return. Going once to see whether it feels right is entirely reasonable. Most people who find Al-Anon useful do so after attending several meetings - the first is rarely the most representative of what the programme offers.
