How Long Does It Take to Break an Addiction

One of the most common questions in addiction treatment is also the hardest to answer simply. The timeline of recovery is not linear, not predictable, and not the same for everyone. But the research tells us a great deal about what to expect.

6 minute read · Updated 2026

Key takeaways

  • Addiction is a chronic condition that is managed rather than cured - recovery is a process measured in years, not a moment when addiction stops.
  • Acute withdrawal timelines vary significantly by substance - ranging from 3-7 days for alcohol to several weeks for benzodiazepines without medical management.
  • Post-acute withdrawal syndrome (PAWS) can persist for months or years after initial detox, with mood instability and cravings returning weeks later.
  • A 28-day residential program establishes the foundation for recovery but does not complete the process - brain and psychological recovery continues 12-18 months.
  • Probability of sustained remission increases substantially after 12-18 months of abstinence, with relapse risk dropping to roughly 15 percent at the two-year mark.

Why "breaking" an addiction is the wrong frame

The question contains a premise worth examining. Addiction is not a habit that gets broken - a stick snapped cleanly in two, with before and after. The clinical consensus, supported by three decades of neuroimaging and longitudinal research, is that addiction is a chronic condition that is managed rather than cured. Like hypertension or type 2 diabetes, it can go into sustained remission. It can cease to define or constrain someone's life. The underlying neurological changes that produced it may diminish substantially. But the expectation of a definitive moment at which the addiction simply stops - as if a lock has clicked open - does not reflect how the brain actually works.

This is not pessimism. It is precision. Understanding recovery as a process rather than an event leads to better decisions about treatment, better management of expectations, and better outcomes. The person who understands that recovery is a long arc is more likely to build the ongoing support structures that make that arc possible.

The acute withdrawal phase

The first phase of physical recovery is acute withdrawal - the period during which the body adjusts to the absence of the substance. The timeline varies substantially by substance:

  • Alcohol: 3–7 days. Can include seizures and delirium tremens in severe cases - one of the few withdrawal syndromes that is directly life-threatening without medical management.
  • Opioids: 5–10 days. Intensely uncomfortable but rarely life-threatening. Symptoms include muscle pain, nausea, anxiety, and insomnia.
  • Benzodiazepines: Highly variable; can extend to several weeks in long-term users. Like alcohol, medically managed taper is essential to avoid seizure risk.
  • Cocaine and stimulants: 1–3 weeks of psychological withdrawal - fatigue, depression, and intense cravings. Physical symptoms are less dramatic than depressant withdrawal.
  • Cannabis: Mild physical withdrawal lasting 1–2 weeks in heavy users - sleep disruption, irritability, appetite changes.

Acute withdrawal is the period managed by medical detox. It is the most acute phase of physical discomfort, but it is also the shortest. What follows is often more clinically challenging.

Post-acute withdrawal syndrome (PAWS)

Following the resolution of acute withdrawal, a significant proportion of people experience a prolonged period of sub-acute symptoms collectively termed post-acute withdrawal syndrome, or PAWS. These symptoms - mood instability, cognitive fog, sleep disruption, difficulty with concentration, anxiety, and recurring cravings - can persist for months, and in some cases years, after the last use. Research from NIDA on addiction science highlights the neurological basis of these extended symptoms.

PAWS is one of the most underappreciated aspects of early recovery and a significant driver of relapse. People who feel substantially better after detox and the first weeks of treatment may be caught off guard by the return of symptoms weeks or months later. Understanding PAWS as a normal and expected part of the recovery process - rather than evidence that something has gone wrong - is important for maintaining engagement with ongoing support.

The psychological timeline

The neurological timelines above describe what happens to the brain as a physical organ. The psychological timeline - the process of rebuilding identity, relationships, and life structures without the substance - is a longer and more individual process that does not reduce to a simple timeframe.

Psychotherapy in recovery addresses the drivers of the addiction - the underlying trauma, attachment patterns, emotional regulation deficits, and cognitive distortions that made the substance so appealing and made stopping so difficult. This work takes time. For people with complex trauma histories, it may continue for years and does not conclude at a defined endpoint.

What 28 days actually achieves

The standard 28-day residential treatment programme is not arbitrary, but it should not be misunderstood as comprehensive. Twenty-eight days is enough time to complete medical detox, stabilise physically, begin the therapeutic work, develop some initial psychological skills, and lay the foundation for ongoing recovery. It is not enough time to complete the recovery process, which - as the timelines above show - plays out over months and years.

The value of residential treatment lies in the quality of the foundation it establishes, and in the aftercare plan it produces. A 28-day programme that leads directly into a structured aftercare plan - ongoing therapy, peer support, relapse prevention work - is significantly more effective than one that does not.

The longer view

The research on long-term recovery outcomes consistently shows that the probability of sustained remission increases substantially after 12 to 18 months of abstinence. At the two-year mark, the probability of relapse drops to roughly 15 percent. At five years, the risk profile of someone in recovery is broadly comparable to someone who has never had an addiction. According to NIAAA research on alcohol use disorder, sustained abstinence and engagement with support are key predictors of long-term outcomes.

These figures should be understood as approximations - they vary by substance, severity, and the presence of co-occurring conditions. But the direction of travel is clear: time matters, and the first year is the most critical.

Why ongoing support matters

Given the timelines above, the importance of ongoing support after residential treatment is not a supplement to recovery - it is a core component of it. The brain changes that treatment begins continue to develop over months and years. The psychological work deepens. The life rebuilding that recovery requires - relationships, employment, purpose, community - is slow work. All of it benefits from structure, accountability, and community that extends well beyond the discharge date. Good aftercare is the scaffolding that makes sustained recovery possible.
