Understanding Prescription Drug Dependency

When medication prescribed in good faith becomes a problem

Prescription drug dependency is one of the most misunderstood forms of addiction. Unlike illegal drug use, it typically begins in a doctor's surgery, with a legitimate prescription for a genuine medical condition. Pain management, anxiety, insomnia, post-surgical recovery, these are the circumstances in which many people find themselves dependent on medication months or years later.

The shame and confusion this creates is profound. People affected often tell themselves: "I am not an addict. I have a prescription." But the physiological reality of dependency does not distinguish between prescribed and non-prescribed substances. The brain and body adapt in the same way, and stopping becomes just as difficult.

Commonly implicated medications include opioid painkillers such as tramadol, codeine, oxycodone, and fentanyl patches; sleeping tablets such as zopiclone and zolpidem; and anxiolytics such as diazepam and alprazolam. Some of these require medically supervised withdrawal. Others can be tapered under clinical guidance.

A particular pattern is seen in professional women in their forties and fifties: a prescription for anxiety or sleep that has continued for years, a gradual escalation in dose, and a growing sense of being trapped with no clear way out. Our programme is experienced in working sensitively with this presentation.

Why GPs Often Miss This

The gap between prescription and recognition

Note on Benzodiazepine and Opioid Withdrawal

If your prescribed medication includes benzodiazepines (diazepam, alprazolam, lorazepam) or opioid painkillers, withdrawal must be medically supervised. Do not attempt to stop abruptly. Please read our dedicated pages on benzodiazepine addiction and opiate addiction for further detail.

General practitioners are often not well placed to recognise or address prescription drug dependency. Consultations are short, repeat prescriptions are easy to issue, and patients are adept at minimising or concealing the extent of their use. Many people have spent years returning for prescription renewals without the dependency ever being named or addressed.

In a residential setting, there is no such constraint. Your treatment team has time and expertise to understand the full picture, address the underlying conditions that drove the original prescription, and create a genuinely personalised plan for recovery.

The residential environment in Sri Lanka also provides something that outpatient treatment cannot: complete physical separation from the pharmacy, the familiar routine, and the subtle daily triggers that sustain prescription drug dependency at home.

Recognising the Signs

Signs that prescription drug use has become dependency

Because prescription drugs come from a doctor, it can be genuinely difficult to recognise when use has crossed into dependency. These signs are worth considering honestly.

Taking more than prescribed

Consistently using a higher dose than prescribed, taking doses more frequently than directed, or finishing a monthly prescription in two weeks are clear indicators that the medication is being used beyond its therapeutic intent.

Anxiety when supplies run low

Significant distress, panic, or preoccupation when the prescription is due for renewal, or when tablets are running low, suggests psychological and physical dependency rather than simple therapeutic use.

Obtaining medication from multiple sources

Visiting more than one GP, using online pharmacies, obtaining medication from friends or relatives, or purchasing online without prescription are all signs that the dependency has moved beyond medical management.

Concealing use from family or doctors

Hiding medication, being evasive when asked about it, or actively misrepresenting consumption to a doctor suggests an awareness that the use has become problematic, even if that is not yet being acknowledged.

Inability to function without the medication

When going a day without the medication is genuinely impossible due to withdrawal symptoms or overwhelming anxiety, dependency is established. This is a physiological state, not a personal weakness.

The original problem no longer responds to the dose

When the pain, anxiety, or sleep problem no longer improves with the medication and more is needed to achieve even partial relief, tolerance has developed and dependency treatment is needed.

The Treatment Journey

How prescription drug dependency treatment works

Treatment is tailored to the specific medication involved and the individual circumstances of each client. The following steps represent the typical pathway.

01

Comprehensive medical and psychological assessment

On arrival, a thorough assessment covers your medication history, current doses, duration of use, any co-occurring mental or physical health conditions, and your personal goals for treatment. This assessment directly informs every element of your programme.

02

Medically supervised detox or taper, where required

For benzodiazepines and opioid painkillers, a carefully managed taper protocol is used to reduce the dose gradually under medical supervision. This minimises discomfort and eliminates the risk of dangerous withdrawal. For other medications, detox may be simpler, but always remains medically overseen.

03

Addressing the original condition therapeutically

Many clients were prescribed medication to manage anxiety, depression, or chronic pain. Therapy sessions address these underlying conditions directly, using evidence-based approaches that do not involve medication dependency. This is essential for sustainable long-term recovery.

04

Rebuilding identity and self-compassion

The shame and self-blame that accompany prescription drug dependency are often significant barriers to recovery. A core part of the therapeutic work involves re-framing the dependency accurately, as a medical consequence rather than a moral failure, and rebuilding a sense of self that is not defined by it.

05

Relapse prevention and discharge planning

Before leaving, clients develop a practical plan for managing health needs without dependency, including alternative strategies for sleep, anxiety, and pain management. Communication with a GP or specialist at home can be facilitated with your consent to ensure continuity of care.

Common Questions

Questions about prescription drug treatment in Sri Lanka

My doctor prescribed this medication. Does that mean I am not really addicted?
The origin of the prescription does not change the physiological reality of dependency. Many people with prescription drug dependency feel ambivalent about applying the word "addiction" to their situation, and that is completely understandable. What matters is whether you are able to stop comfortably, whether the medication is still serving its original purpose, and whether it is causing problems in your life. If the honest answer to any of those questions is troubling, it is worth seeking specialist advice.
Will my employer or family need to know?
Your treatment is completely confidential. You decide what information, if any, is shared with anyone outside the treatment team. Many clients arrange their time in Sri Lanka without disclosing the reason to colleagues or extended family, framing it simply as a period of medical leave or a private wellness retreat. We can advise on how to handle this sensitively.
I have a genuine medical condition. What happens to my pain or anxiety management after treatment?
This is a central concern that is addressed directly in your treatment plan. The therapeutic work includes developing non-pharmaceutical approaches to managing the underlying conditions, and, where ongoing medication is clinically appropriate, ensuring it is a different class of medication without dependency risk. No client leaves without a clear, medically sound plan for managing their health needs.
How long will I need to stay?
The length of stay depends on the medication involved, the duration and severity of the dependency, and the depth of the psychological work required. A minimum of 28 days is recommended. For long-term benzodiazepine or opioid dependency, 60 days often allows a more thorough taper and more complete therapeutic work. An honest assessment of your situation will be provided at the start of treatment.
I feel embarrassed to be in a rehab centre alongside people with street drug problems.
This concern is common, and we understand it. In practice, the shared experience of dependency, and the shared courage it takes to seek help, tends to dissolve these distinctions quickly. Our clients come from very different backgrounds and with very different substances, and most find the diversity of the group therapeutic rather than uncomfortable. You will not be defined by your medication history here.
