What Happens to Your Body During Alcohol Withdrawal

Stopping alcohol after heavy, prolonged use is not simply a matter of willpower. It is a medical event - and for some people, a dangerous one. Understanding what happens and why is the first step towards getting safe help.

8 minute read · Medically reviewed

Why alcohol withdrawal is different

Most people know that stopping heroin or cocaine is difficult. Fewer realise that alcohol withdrawal can be significantly more dangerous than withdrawal from either of those substances. Unlike opioids, which cause intense suffering but are rarely fatal, alcohol withdrawal can kill - and does, when it is not properly managed.

This is not said to frighten you. It is said because it matters. People who have been drinking heavily for months or years, who decide they are going to stop on their own, sometimes do not understand the level of risk involved. This article explains what is happening inside the body, what to expect, and why medical supervision is not optional for many people - it is essential.

Important: If you or someone you know drinks heavily every day and is planning to stop, please speak with a doctor before doing so. Alcohol withdrawal can cause seizures and, in severe cases, a life-threatening condition called delirium tremens. Do not attempt unsupervised detox if you are a heavy daily drinker.

What alcohol does to the brain

To understand withdrawal, you first need to understand what alcohol does to the brain over time.

Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant. It works primarily by enhancing the effect of GABA - the brain's main inhibitory neurotransmitter - while simultaneously suppressing glutamate, the main excitatory neurotransmitter. In plain language: alcohol quiets the brain down. It slows things. That is why it reduces anxiety, loosens inhibitions, and makes you feel relaxed.

The problem is what happens with prolonged exposure. The brain is extraordinarily adaptive. When it detects that it is being constantly suppressed, it compensates. It downregulates GABA receptors - making them less sensitive - and upregulates glutamate activity to counteract the dampening effect. Over weeks and months, the brain essentially rewires itself to function normally with alcohol present.

When alcohol is then removed, the brain is left in a state of extreme excitation. GABA suppression is gone. Glutamate is firing freely. The nervous system goes into overdrive. That is alcohol withdrawal.

The timeline: what happens and when

Alcohol withdrawal follows a reasonably predictable timeline, though the severity varies enormously depending on how much someone has been drinking, for how long, and individual biological factors.

6–12 hours

Early symptoms begin

The first signs of withdrawal typically appear within six to twelve hours of the last drink. These include anxiety, restlessness, tremors, sweating, nausea, and an elevated heart rate. For many people, this is when the urge to drink again becomes very strong - not because of craving in the usual sense, but because another drink genuinely makes the symptoms go away.

12–24 hours

Symptoms intensify; seizure risk begins

Between twelve and twenty-four hours, symptoms typically intensify. Tremors worsen. Blood pressure rises. Some people develop hallucinations - visual, auditory, or tactile - while still remaining broadly conscious. This is called alcoholic hallucinosis. Crucially, the risk of seizure begins in this window. Withdrawal seizures are generalised tonic-clonic seizures and can occur without warning.

24–72 hours

Peak risk - delirium tremens

This is the most dangerous phase. For the majority of people, symptoms will begin to stabilise by 48 to 72 hours. But for a significant minority - around 5% - this is when delirium tremens can develop. DTs involve profound confusion, fever, severe autonomic instability, and a mortality rate of up to 15% without medical treatment. With proper medical care, that figure falls dramatically.

3–7 days

Gradual stabilisation

For those who have passed through the acute phase safely, symptoms begin to reduce from around day three. Sleep remains disturbed. Anxiety and low mood persist. Physical symptoms gradually ease. By the end of the first week, most people are physically stable, though far from comfortable.

Weeks 2–4+

Post-acute withdrawal

A significant number of people experience post-acute withdrawal syndrome (PAWS) - a prolonged period of mood disturbance, sleep difficulty, cognitive fog, and persistent anxiety that can last weeks or months. This is not dangerous in the same way as acute withdrawal, but it is a significant contributor to relapse if not understood and managed.

Who is most at risk?

Not everyone who stops drinking will experience severe withdrawal. Risk is higher in people who:

  • Have been drinking heavily every day for months or years
  • Drink more than 8 to 10 units per day - roughly a bottle of wine or four to five pints
  • Have a history of previous withdrawal seizures or delirium tremens
  • Have gone through multiple previous detoxes - each successive withdrawal can become more severe, a phenomenon known as kindling
  • Have underlying health conditions, particularly liver disease, cardiac issues, or nutritional deficiencies

The kindling effect: Each time a person goes through alcohol withdrawal, the brain's excitatory response becomes more pronounced. Someone on their fifth or sixth detox may experience far more severe symptoms than they did the first time - even drinking the same amount. This is why repeated unsupervised detox attempts carry escalating risk.

What a medically supervised detox involves

A properly managed alcohol detox is not complicated, but it requires clinical oversight. The cornerstone is the use of benzodiazepines - most commonly diazepam or chlordiazepoxide - which work on the same GABA receptors as alcohol and prevent the brain entering a state of dangerous excitation.

In a residential detox programme, medication is prescribed on a reducing schedule over five to seven days. Nursing staff monitor vital signs, assess symptom severity using standardised tools, and adjust dosage accordingly. Thiamine (Vitamin B1) supplementation is given to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy - a serious neurological complication that can develop when heavy drinkers stop and are nutritionally depleted.

Beyond medication, a good detox environment provides regular meals, adequate hydration, a calm low-stimulation setting, emotional support from experienced staff, and preparation for the rehabilitation work that follows. Detox alone is not treatment - it is the medical phase that makes treatment possible.

The psychological dimension

There is a psychological side to withdrawal that is often underestimated. As the sedating effects of alcohol leave the system, emotions that have been numbed for months or years begin to surface. Anxiety is extremely common - sometimes at a level that feels unbearable. Depression frequently emerges. For people who have been using alcohol to manage underlying trauma, the absence of that chemical buffer can be acutely destabilising.

This is not weakness. It is neurochemistry. The brain's reward system - flooded with dopamine signals by alcohol - goes into a state of profound under-activity when alcohol is removed. The world feels flat, joyless, threatening. These feelings typically improve significantly over the first two to four weeks, but they are real and they are one of the primary drivers of relapse in early recovery.

Why people try to stop alone - and why it often fails

People attempt home detox for understandable reasons. They do not want to admit the extent of their drinking to a doctor. They worry about stigma. They want to deal with it quietly. They believe - sincerely - that they have enough willpower to get through it.

What they underestimate is how powerful the physiological drive to drink becomes during withdrawal. When symptoms set in - the shaking, sweating, racing heart, overwhelming anxiety - drinking again does not feel like a choice. It feels like the only way to survive. And it provides almost immediate relief. That relief reinforces the behaviour. The cycle continues.

This is not a failure of character. It is a neurological trap. The path out requires more than willpower - it requires a structured environment, clinical support, and time away from the triggers and pressures that sustain the pattern.

Getting the right help

If you are concerned about your own drinking - or that of someone close to you - the most important thing is to speak to someone who understands the clinical reality of what you are dealing with. Not to be judged. Not to be given a leaflet. To get an honest assessment of the level of risk and the most appropriate path forward.

Residential rehabilitation, particularly in a setting that combines medical detox with structured therapeutic support, produces significantly better long-term outcomes than community detox for people with severe alcohol dependency. The residential environment removes the person from their triggers, provides continuity of care, and allows the genuine work of recovery to begin.
