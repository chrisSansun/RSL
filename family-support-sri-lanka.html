For families

Supporting a family member through rehab

Watching someone you love struggle with addiction is one of the hardest experiences a family can face. This page is for you.

What families need to know

Your role in recovery matters more than you know

If you are reading this page, you are probably exhausted. You may have been managing this situation for months or years. You have probably tried to help in every way you could think of, and some of those things may have worked briefly before something pulled it back. That is what addiction does. It is not a reflection of your love or your effort.

The most important thing to understand is that your loved one's recovery is not solely your responsibility, and it cannot be achieved by love alone. Recovery requires professional treatment, personal commitment from the person in treatment, and a supportive environment when they return home. Your role matters enormously in that last part, and this page will help you understand how to play it well.

Supporting without enabling

The line between supporting someone and enabling their addiction is one of the hardest things for families to navigate. Enabling is not about being weak or loving too much. It is about the way that certain kinds of support, however well-intentioned, make it easier for someone to continue using than to face the consequences of their use.

Common enabling behaviours include covering for someone at work when they cannot function, giving money that you know will be used for substances, making excuses for their behaviour to friends and family, or repeatedly rescuing them from consequences. None of these things come from a bad place. They come from love and a desire to protect someone. But they can inadvertently remove the pressure that might otherwise motivate someone to seek help.

The alternative is not to withdraw your love or create dramatic confrontations. It is to be clear, consistent, and honest about what you will and will not do. This is very difficult to do alone. Family therapy, Al-Anon, and Families Anonymous all exist to help families develop this kind of clarity without sacrificing the relationship.

What happens when they are in treatment

The first week in residential treatment can feel unsettling for families, particularly when contact is limited while your loved one settles in. This period of reduced contact is normal and clinically advised. It gives the person in treatment the space to begin engaging with the programme without the complicated emotions of home being immediately present. It is not a sign that something is wrong.

After the initial settling-in period, most programmes allow regular phone or video contact. Family therapy sessions via video call are available in most programmes and are something we strongly encourage. The goal is not to process family dynamics in a clinical setting for its own sake, but to begin preparing for the reality that the person coming home will be different, and that the family system they are returning to needs to adapt to support that change.

What to do while they are away

This period is an opportunity. If you have been living in the anxiety and chaos of someone else's addiction for a long time, you may have forgotten what your own needs look like. Use this time to reconnect with your own life, your own health, and your own support. Al-Anon and Families Anonymous are free, widely available, and genuinely helpful for family members. Your own therapist, if you do not already have one, can be invaluable.

It is also worth beginning to think practically about what the home environment will look like when they return. Are there substances in the house? Are there social arrangements that will need to change? Are there conversations that need to happen before they come back? The aftercare process will address these questions in a structured way, but your early thinking matters.

When they come home

The transition back from residential treatment is one of the highest-risk periods in recovery. Your loved one will be returning to an environment that holds many of the associations and triggers connected to their use. They will also be in early recovery, which means their emotional regulation and stress tolerance are not yet what they will become. Patience, consistency, and an understanding of what they are going through will matter more than any specific conversation in the first weeks.

Try to avoid putting them in high-stress situations unnecessarily. Try not to demand immediate resolution of all the things that went wrong during the period of their addiction. Give recovery the space and time it needs. This does not mean never addressing difficult things. It means timing and approach matter enormously.

Key phases

Three phases of your family's journey

Supporting without enabling

The most important shift for most families is learning the difference between support that helps recovery and support that inadvertently sustains dependency. This is not about blame. It is about understanding the dynamics of addiction and making deliberate choices about how you show up.

During treatment

Expect limited contact in the first days. After that, regular updates and the option of family therapy sessions. Use this period to attend to your own wellbeing, connect with Al-Anon or Families Anonymous, and begin thinking about what the home environment needs to look like when they return.

When they come home

The transition home is high-risk. Patience, consistency, and removing obvious triggers matter. Difficult conversations can happen, but timing and approach are everything in early recovery. A structured aftercare plan will help guide this period for the whole family.

Questions families ask

What families want to know

How do I persuade someone to go to rehab?
You cannot force an adult into treatment, and trying to do so through confrontation or ultimatums often backfires. The most effective approach is usually a calm, honest, and consistent conversation about what you have observed and how it has affected you and the family, combined with a clear and compassionate offer of help in the form of a treatment plan that is ready to go. Our page on how to stage an intervention goes into this in detail.
Can I visit during treatment in Sri Lanka?
For programmes of 60 days or longer, visiting is often possible and sometimes encouraged as part of the family therapy component. The timing and format of a visit is discussed with the clinical team. Visits too early in treatment can disrupt the settling-in process. The clinical team will advise on the right time and what a visit should look like to be helpful rather than disruptive.
What do I tell other people while they are away?
That is entirely your choice and your loved one's choice. Many families say simply that the person is taking time out for health reasons. You have no obligation to explain more than you choose to. Treatment is confidential and there is no record created that would be visible to employers, insurance companies, or anyone else without your explicit consent.
What if they relapse after coming home?
Relapse is common, especially in the first year after residential treatment. It does not mean treatment failed or that recovery is impossible. It means the process needs to continue. The right response is not punishment or withdrawal of support, but a calm return to the plan, which may include contacting the treatment team, increasing support, or in some cases returning for further treatment. See our aftercare page for more on this.
How do I look after myself through all of this?
This is not a secondary question. Family members of people with addiction are at significantly elevated risk of their own mental health difficulties, including anxiety, depression, and trauma symptoms. Your own support matters. Al-Anon and Families Anonymous are free and widely available. Many family members find individual therapy enormously helpful. You cannot sustain effective support for someone else if you are running on empty.
